Offset calculator

Inches · answers to nearest ¹⁄₁₆"
What is this? When do I use it?

Use it when a pipe has to move over (or up and over) to dodge something, and you need the length of the angled piece.

Words: offset (rise) is how far the pipe has to move; roll is a second move at 90° to the first (that's a rolling offset); travel is the angled pipe measured fitting center to fitting center; run (setback) is how far down the line the second fitting lands; take-off is how much of the fitting eats into the length.

The idea: it's one right triangle (two for a roll). For 45s, travel = offset × 1.414, every time.

Cut length—The blue pipe. Travel minus both take-offs.
Travel—Fitting center to fitting center.
Run (setback)—How far along the pipe the 2nd fitting sits.
True offset—Straight-line shift: √(rise² + roll²).
Turn fittings—Roll both fittings this far off plumb, toward the roll side, so the cut piece aims at the new pipe.

      Parallel offsets

      Several pipes turning together
      What is this? When do I use it?

      Use it when two or more pipes (hot and cold, a bank of drains) turn together and need to stay evenly spaced through the turn.

      The idea: if every pipe started its turn at the same spot, the inside pipe would pinch toward the outside one. Each pipe's fittings slide forward a set amount (the advance) so the bank stays parallel. The diagram shows which pipe starts first.

      Pipe1st fitting2nd fitting
      Show the math

        WHY The fittings have to sit on a line that splits the turn in half (the dashed lines); that keeps the pipes the same distance apart through the angle. The pipe on the inside of the first turn starts first and comes back first too. Every pipe gets the same travel and the same cut. With 90s, each next fitting moves the full spacing.

            Square it up

            3-4-5 · check a corner · check a box
            What is this? When do I use it?

            Use it when laying out walls, a slab, a tub or shower base, or checking that a room or box is really square before you set flanges and stubs off it.

            The idea: 3² + 4² = 5². If the diagonal reads exactly 5 (or 10, or 15), the corner is 90°. For a whole box: equal diagonals mean square.

            Show the math
              Underground on a slabPull string lines for two walls off the batter boards or forms. Square them with 6-8-10 or bigger before you mark flanges and drains: every stub-up is dimensioned off those lines, so a line out of square walks every stub down the run.
              Two dimensions, two wallsLocate a drain or flange with one dimension off each wall line, each measured square to its own wall. Measure on a slant and the stub lands off its mark.
              Bigger triangle, betterA ¹⁄₁₆" tape miss on the diagonal moves the corner the same ¹⁄₁₆"-ish no matter the size, so it's a smaller angle over longer legs. Use the biggest triangle that fits: 9-12-15 is 3× as true as 3-4-5.
              Rooms aren't squareBefore a tub, shower base or vanity rough, check the room's diagonals (Check a box). Out-of-square walls move where a "centered" drain lands. Split the difference or ask the super which wall to follow.
              Burn an inchWhen the tape hook can't catch a corner, hold the 1" mark on it and subtract 1" from what you read. Easy to forget: say it out loud.
              Square cutsA pipe cut out of square won't bottom in the hub. Wrap a straight piece of paper or tape around the pipe, line its edge up with itself, and mark along it.

              WHY In any right triangle, the two short sides squared add up to the long side squared (Pythagoras): 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5². Double every side and the shape doesn't change, only the size, so 6-8-10 works too. A box with equal opposite sides is a rectangle only when both diagonals match: lean it and one diagonal grows while the other shrinks.

                                  What it isA passive radon system: gravel or a collector mat under the slab, and a pipe from there up through a warm part of the house and out the roof. Common in Utah homes, where radon runs high.

                                  What goes where on the main
                                  What is this? When do I use it?

                                  Read it top to bottom the way water flows: from the meter, through the main valve and PRV, the treatment equipment, then splitting to cold for the house and to the heater for hot.

                                  The idea: the order isn't random. Each piece protects the next one (filter protects softener) or needs to be where it is to work (expansion tank between the heater and whatever closed the system).

                                  1. Main shutoff. Full-port ball valve. Tag it so the homeowner can find it in a flood.
                                  2. PRV + gauge. Required over 80 psi static. Its strainer goes first; a gauge after it shows the set pressure.
                                  3. Tee: unsoftened. Hose bibbs (and irrigation, where it's on culinary water). Don't soften the lawn.
                                  4. Sediment filter. Protects the softener valve and resin.
                                  5. Carbon (optional). Takes out chlorine; the resin lasts longer.
                                  6. Softener + bypass. In service the bypass valve is closed (solid symbol). Drain through an air gap; brine overflow on its own line, by gravity.
                                  7. Tee: soft cold. Cold trunk or manifold for the house.
                                  8. Expansion tank. On the heater's cold, with no valve between it and the heater. Support it.
                                  9. Water heater. Tank or tankless, with a shutoff on the cold.
                                  10. Mixing valve (optional). ASSE 1017, when the tank is kept hot (140°F).
                                  11. Recirc return. Comes back to the heater through a check valve so it can't flow backward.

                                  Heat pump water heaters

                                  The 2029 federal rule
                                  The ruleElectric tanks over 35 gal made after May 6, 2029 must be heat pumps in practice. A small electric class (20–35 gal, limited first-hour rating) can stay plain resistance, so expect 35-gal tanks sold as "40s" for tight closets. Gas tanks only get a small efficiency bump. This storage rule (DOE, 89 FR 37778, May 6, 2024) is still in place Oct 2026. The separate gas tankless rule was voided by Congress in May 2025 (H.J.Res. 20).
                                  Tax creditThe federal heat pump water heater credit ended Dec 31, 2025. A customer quoting it has old info: ask Rocky Mountain Power or Enbridge for current rebates.
                                  AirIt pulls heat from room air: about 700 ft³ of space for most 240V units (some 450), or a louvered door or ducting per the maker. Best in a 50–90°F space; in a cold Utah garage it leans on the backup element.
                                  CondensateIt makes water. Plan a condensate drain (or pump) at rough-in, and set the unit level so it drains.
                                  ElectricUsually 240V / 30A, like a regular electric tank. 120V plug-in models exist for gas swaps, sized up about two tank sizes.
                                  Cold air & noiseBlows cool air and hums like a fridge (≈ 45–55 dB): keep it away from thermostats and bedrooms. Seismic straps still apply.

                                  A heat-pump-ready spot = enough air (or a duct path), a drain within reach, and a 240V circuit. Cheap at rough-in, expensive later.

                                  Water heater output

                                  Recovery · tankless flow
                                  What is this? When do I use it?

                                  Use it when checking whether a heater can keep up: a tank's recovery, or how many gpm a tankless makes in winter.

                                  The idea: heating water takes a fixed amount of energy per degree (1 BTU per pound per °F). More rise (colder incoming water) means fewer gallons from the same burner. That's why tankless output drops in a Utah January.

                                  Recovery—gph = BTU/h × eff ÷ (8.34 × rise)
                                  Temp rise—Set point − incoming.
                                  Show the math

                                    Plan on 40°F incoming water in a Utah winter (Rheem's northern-state guidance). Tank sizing goes by first-hour rating (gallons it can deliver in the busiest hour, on the label), not just tank size. Tankless: add up the gpm of fixtures that run at the same time.

                                      Softener install

                                      Loop, bypass, drain · the manual wins
                                      UTSoftener loopCommon in Utah new builds: the main comes into the mech room, loops out and back so a softener drops in later. Cap it or jump it with a bypass until then. Leave power and a drain within reach.
                                      BypassNeeded so the house keeps water while the softener's out. Use the valve's bypass or a 3-valve loop (in, out, bypass). In service, the bypass is closed.
                                      PressureAbout 20–125 psi at the valve (Fleck 5600SXT). Low pressure means weak backwash and a weak brine draw.
                                      Hot waterInlet water ≤ 110°F. Heat from the water heater backs up the cold line, so don't hang the softener right off the heater's cold inlet.
                                      Drain line½" minimum; ¾" if the run is over 20 ft or backwash is over 7 gpm (Fleck). Not too long, not too high, no smaller than the maker says.
                                      Air gapThe drain ends above the standpipe or floor drain with an air gap, never shoved down into it. WHY A backed-up drain could siphon into the house water.
                                      Brine overflowIts own line to the drain by gravity, lower than the overflow fitting. Never tee it into the valve's drain line.
                                      SolderSweat joints before you thread on the valve; keep heat ≥ 6" from the drain flow control (Fleck). Plastic parts melt.
                                      PowerA full-time 120V outlet: not on a switch, so the clock and meter keep time.
                                      LocationIndoors, no freezing, level floor, not below a sewer line, out of direct sun.
                                      StartupBypass on, flush the new pipe, then ease it back to service. Water about 1" over the brine grid before salt (Fleck). Run a manual regen and test the water.

                                      Code: the softener drain is an indirect waste (air gap). NSF/ANSI 44 limits a softener to 15 psi loss at rated flow; undersized ones choke flow.

                                      Grains a day—People × gallons × hardness

                                        Softener service

                                        Settings · salt · trouble
                                        Hardness settingThe tested gpg (plus iron, if any). Set too low → hard water by day 5. Set too high → salt and water wasted.
                                        Metered vs timerMetered (demand) valves count gallons and regen when the resin's used up. Timer valves regen on set days no matter what: old-school, wasteful on a light week.
                                        Regen timeUsually 2 a.m.: nobody's using water. Hard water flows to the house during regen.
                                        Salt levelKeep salt above the water in the brine tank. Pellets or crystals; skip rock salt (dirt).
                                        Salt bridgeA crust over an empty space: looks full, makes no brine, water goes hard. Push a broom handle down and break it.
                                        PotassiumPotassium chloride works for people avoiding sodium. Costs more; the setting usually needs bumping up. Check the manual.
                                        Resin lifeChlorine in city water slowly breaks resin down. A carbon filter before it, or 10% crosslink resin, buys years. Utah County systems checked use free chlorine.
                                        SodiumSoftened water adds a little sodium. Fine for most people; someone on a low-sodium diet asks their doctor, or gets an RO tap.
                                        Not a fix forBacteria, lead, arsenic, nitrate, chlorine taste. Those need filters or RO.

                                        Not softeners

                                        Salt-free, descalers, ionizers
                                        Salt-free (TAC)Media turns hardness into tiny crystals that ride through instead of sticking as scale. The water stays hard: no slick feel, spots still dry on glass. No salt, no drain, no power. Tested by the German DVGW W512 method; no NSF standard for it. Iron and manganese foul it, so they come out first.
                                        Magnetic / electronicClamp-on coils or magnets that claim to stop scale. Results are mixed at best. Don't sell one as a softener.
                                        Water ionizersCountertop or under-sink "alkaline" units: electrolysis plates split water into an alkaline stream to drink and an acidic stream to waste. Plumb a cold feed and a waste line. The health claims don't hold up: your body keeps its own pH. Hard water scales the plates, so they usually sit after a softener or filter.
                                        Deionizers (DI)Mixed-bed resin that pulls out nearly all minerals: spot-free rinse for cars and windows, aquariums, labs. Resin gets swapped or recharged, not salted. Not for a whole house.
                                        On the jobTreat them like any treatment unit: shutoffs, bypass, a drain with an air gap if it has one, and the maker's manual.

                                        Recirculation

                                        Hot water without the wait
                                        Dedicated returnA third line from the far fixture back to the heater. Pump at the heater, check valve on the return. Best result; plan it at rough-in.

                                        Trim out a shower / tub valve

                                        Do it in this order
                                        1. Flush the lines first. Run water through the valve with the test cap or flush plug off, before the cartridge goes in. Rough-in debris is the #1 cartridge killer.
                                        2. Check wall depth. Pull the plaster guard and confirm the finished wall is inside the valve's depth range. Too deep needs the maker's extension kit; too shallow and the handle won't seat.
                                        3. Cartridge in, hot on the left. Follow the trim's sheet for orientation and the retaining clip or bonnet nut.
                                        4. Set the limit stop. Run full hot, put a thermometer in the stream, set the stop so it tops out at 120°F or less (ASSE 1016 / 1070). WHY The stop limits how far the handle turns, not the temperature. In January the cold line runs about 40°F and cools the mix; in July it's warmer, so the same stop runs hotter. Set a few degrees under 120°F, and recheck if you set it in winter.
                                        5. Hot and cold backwards? Fix it at the cartridge, not the handle. Moen 1222: rotate the stem 180°. Others: follow the maker's reversal steps. WHY Flipping just the handle leaves the limit stop on the wrong side, so it no longer caps the hot.
                                        6. Tub spout. Match the stub-out to the spout (slip-on vs ½" IPS threaded, length per its sheet). Delta: spout 8"–18" below the valve or you get shower rise (water creeps out the head). Most makers want metal, not PEX, to a diverter spout.
                                        7. Escutcheon. Gasket or caulk per the sheet. Many makers say leave the bottom unsealed so any leak shows instead of rotting the wall.
                                        8. Test. Every function, every diverter position, look behind the escutcheon for drips, then a flush-test: someone flushes a toilet while you feel the temperature.

                                        Match trim to the valve

                                        Read the valve first
                                        The ruleTrim has to match the brand and the valve family already in the wall. A Moen trim won't go on a Delta valve, and even inside one brand most families don't cross (Moen Posi-Temp trim won't fit an M-CORE valve).
                                        ID the valveBrand and model cast or printed on the body, the plaster guard or the test cap. The box or spec sheet at the house. The builder's fixture selection sheet. Pull a cartridge and read its stamp.
                                        Where's the cartridge?Some families ship it in the valve (Moen Posi-Temp, Kohler Rite-Temp). Others ship it with the trim (Moen M-CORE, Delta MultiChoice), so a "universal" valve becomes a pressure-balance or thermostatic shower depending on the trim you buy.
                                        WHYPressure balanceA spool slides to keep hot and cold pressures equal. Someone flushes, cold pressure drops, the spool cuts hot too, so nobody gets scalded. One handle = temperature (and sometimes volume). ASSE 1016.
                                        WHYThermostaticA wax or bimetal element senses the actual water temperature and mixes to hold it. Usually lets you set volume separately. Costs more, holds temperature tighter.

                                        How to ID any part

                                        Do it in this order
                                        1. Photo it before you touch it. Logo, front, back, underside, any label, and the part next to a tape measure. Once it's apart, the photos are your only record of how it went together.
                                        2. Find the brand. Logo on the handle, cap, escutcheon, spout or body. WHY Brand narrows everything: a Moen cartridge will never fit a Delta valve, even if they look alike.
                                        3. Find a number. See Where the number lives below. Numbers like "A112.18.1" or "ASSE 1016" are standards, not model numbers.
                                        4. Pull the worn part and read its stamp. Cartridges, stems and flappers are usually stamped. A stamp can be a component number rather than the order number; the maker's tech line can translate it.
                                        5. Measure what you can't read. Length, OD, spline count on the stem, thread size (see Threads & sizes). Write it on the photo.
                                        6. Search the maker's part finder by model, or call their tech line with the photos, model and serial (Trim → Finders).
                                        7. Take the old part to the counter. Matching side by side beats any description. Ask for their cartridge or stem identifier book.

                                        New construction shortcut: the builder's fixture selection sheet lists every model in the house. Get it once per subdivision and save it in My files.

                                          Leak or just cold?

                                          Air-test gauge vs. temperature
                                          What is this? When do I use it?

                                          Use it when an air test lost pressure overnight and you're not sure if it's a leak or just the house cooling down.

                                          The idea: air shrinks when it cools, so the gauge drops even with no leak. Enter the start pressure and the temperatures; it tells you what the gauge should read from cold alone. Lower than that = look for a leak.

                                          Cold alone would read—Same air, new temperature.
                                          Verdict—Enter what the gauge reads now.
                                          Show the math

                                            WHY Trapped air shrinks as it cools (gas law on absolute pressure: gauge + air pressure, ≈12.5 psi at 4,500 ft). A shell that cools 30°F overnight drops an 80 psi test about 5 psi with no leak at all, and a 5 psi DWV test about 1 psi, which is 20% of it. Sun on the pipe pushes it the other way. Read the gauge at the same temperature you set it at, or let this do the math. Still dropping more than this says? Soap the joints.

                                            Callback killers

                                            Prove it, then protect it
                                            Screws & nailsMost simple leak callbacks come from trades who work after you, and the hole may not leak until the house moves. Plate anything under 1¼" from the face, mark plate spots for the drywall crew, photograph your plates. WHY With no plate and a normal-length screw, the plumber usually pays for the repair (Copper Development Assn.).
                                            Baseline testBefore cover: gauge photo with the time and psi. Retest at final and compare. That photo settles arguments.
                                            Wall photosEvery wall and ceiling after rough, before insulation, keyed to a floor-plan number. Find pipes later without opening drywall.
                                            PEX expansion (F1960)Square cut. Ring to the stop. Insert right after the last expansion; you should feel resistance (none = over-expanded).                                             Habits that save callbacks

                                            From the trade, checked against the makers
                                            "UP" goes upBefore you strap a shower valve, find UP on the body and put it on top: tub port down, hot on the left (Delta and Moen rough sheets). WHY Upside down swaps the head and spout, and the fix is opening the tile.
                                            Bonnet off to sweatPull the bonnet and test cap (and stops on "with stops" bodies) before you solder a valve. Flush after the test, before the cartridge goes in (Delta R10000). WHY Torch heat cooks the seals; you find out at trim.
                                            Cap countAt rough, write down every vent cap, test ball and plug. At final the count is zero. Look up at every roof vent from the street. WHY A capped stack still drains, so nobody notices until traps gurgle. Trade practice.
                                            Read the plugCheck the test plug's back-pressure rating before you fill: 10 ft of head is about 4.3 psi, and some pillow plugs are rated 8 ft. Block plugs on air tests (Cherne). WHY A plug that lets go floods the trench and hits whoever's in front of it.
                                            Turn, then holdCut square, bevel, deburr. Dry fit: ⅓–⅔ of the way in before it's tight. Prime PVC until the surface softens (scrape test) (ABS needs no primer). Push to the stop with a ¼ turn, then hold 30 s, longer in cold or on big pipe (Weld-On). WHY Let go early and it backs out: a short, starved joint.
                                            Keep drains closedRough to trim: cap or tape every tub, shower, floor and sink drain and the toilet flanges. At trim, run every drain hard and flush each toilet 3 times. WHY Mud and grout set in traps; the first clog in a new house is yours. Trade practice.
                                            Shower arm solidDrop-ear elbow screwed to blocking, square to the wall. Valve and arm stubs in copper or on a bracket, not loose PEX. WHY A loose ell spins when the arm is threaded, and leaks inside the wall. Trade practice.
                                            Cabinets & baseboardGet the cabinet sheet before you stub lavs and sinks: drawer bases need stubs clear of the drawers. Ask the baseboard height and put the toilet supply fully above it. Trade practice.
                                            Roof before shinglesGet vents through the deck before the roofer shingles. Late? Call the roofer to flash it: don't cut finished roofing. WHY The plumber who cuts a finished roof usually owns the leak.
                                            Let hot PEX moveDrill a little oversize, isolators at metal, don't clamp hot lines tight, loop long runs. Keep PEX off the tops of joists where flooring gets nailed. WHY PEX that binds ticks in the wall; a nail in PEX can hold for years, then leak.
                                            Walk it before trimBefore you set anything, photo chipped tubs, broken or buried stubs, mud in drains and missing access panels, and send them to your lead and the super that day. WHY Damage found later gets pinned on whoever was last in the house.
                                            Stone topsGranite, marble, quartz, cultured marble: stain-free putty or the sink sheet's sealant. Regular putty is oil-based and can ring the drain.
                                            Flood the overflowFill the tub into the overflow for a few minutes and check below. Snug the plate evenly. WHY The overflow gasket only leaks in a full tub, into the ceiling below.

                                            Sources: Delta R10000 and Moen INS2153D rough sheets, Cherne test-plug catalog, Weld-On, Oatey, InterNACHI inspector forums, HeatingHelp, Fine Homebuilding, the BC open plumbing-apprentice text, Beehive Plumbing (Utah). Your shop's standard wins.

                                            Secondary water (PI)

                                            Very Utah
                                            What it isPressurized irrigation: untreated canal or reservoir water for lawns, in its own pipes. Separate box and meter near the front property line.
                                            The hard ruleNever connect secondary to culinary (drinking) water, not even with a valve (Utah Code 19-4-112). That's a cross-connection.
                                            WHYWhy care?It's untreated and can carry E. coli and fertilizer (Lehi). One wrong hookup and a whole neighborhood's drinking water can get it.
                                            Who has itUtah County, confirmed on city sites: Lehi, Saratoga Springs, Eagle Mountain, Spanish Fork, Springville. American Fork likely. Orem: no citywide PI. Elsewhere: ask.
                                            Tell them apartPurple = irrigation / non-potable. Blue = potable. Green = sewer (APWA). Springville: purple PE tubing, purple "non-potable" tape about 12" over the pipe, tracer wire, 30" cover, green IRRIGATION box in the park strip. Saratoga: green box near the property line, usually a 1" ball valve.
                                            Field rulesSeparate trench from the water service (Eagle Mountain doesn't allow sharing). Nothing but purple past the PI box. Never a hose bibb off PI at the house. Tag PI valves.
                                            PressureCan run 40–125 psi depending on lot elevation (Springville). If it's more than the sprinklers need, add a reducer on the irrigation side.
                                            SeasonAbout mid-April to mid-October. Saratoga drains the week of Oct 15; Lehi wants connections off by Oct 15.
                                            MetersState law: every secondary connection metered by Jan 1, 2030 (Utah Code 73-10-34). New lots may already get one.
                                            UTDrinking-water backupIf potable water backs up a PI system: an RP right after the potable connection, then a dual-source connection so only one supply can flow. Tested within 10 days of install and every year. RP at least 12" above grade with 12" clear around it, horizontal unless listed otherwise, never in a pit or piped straight to a drain (Utah amendments 1301.5, 608.1.2.1).
                                            InspectionSome cities inspect the sprinkler-to-PI connection separately: Herriman lists it; Springville charges $50 for the switch-over.
                                            2026 droughtStatewide drought emergency declared May 21, 2026. Watering rules come from the city or water district, not the state. Weber Basin cut outdoor water 20%; Roy PI shut off by Oct 1, 2026.
                                            Saratoga tiersAll PI is metered. In a shortage, overuse runs from $0.35 up to $8.00 per 1,000 gal. A PI leak shows up on the owner's bill: fix it before you leave.

                                            Buy good or buy cheap?

                                            Spend where failure costs you
                                            Buy goodTools that measure, hold or hurt when they fail: tape, levels, pliers, pipe wrenches, knee pads, ladders, anything you use all day.
                                            Buy cheapTools that get lost, lent or abused: markers, mini hacksaws, files, extra knives, scrapers, a "beater" set for the truck.
                                            Borrow or rentOnce-a-year tools: snap cutter, core drill, pipe threader, big hammer drill. Home centers and rental yards rent by the day.
                                            One batteryPick one cordless brand and stay on it. Batteries are the expensive part; bare tools are cheap once you own packs. Your shop's brand is often the smart pick: you can borrow chargers.
                                            WHYCheap twiceA $15 tool you replace three times costs more than a $40 one with a lifetime warranty. Count replacements, not the sticker.
                                            Start smallBuy the basics, then buy the tool you wished you had on the job. That's how you learn which ones matter.

                                            Tool trade-offs

                                            Honest pros and cons
                                            Crimp vs expansionCrimp: any PEX, cheap tools, but a smaller fitting bore and every ring needs the gauge. Expansion (F1960): full flow, no gauge, PEX-a only, slower to seal under 40°F. Both last when done right; price and local supply decide.
                                            Push-fitNo tool, costs more, wrap it before burial. Carry a few for emergencies; most shops don't rough a house with them.
                                            M12 vs M18 expanderM12: ⅜–1", light for cabinets, RAPID SEAL heads for cold work. M18: ⅜–1½", heavier. Uponor heads fit both but won't auto-rotate. Many pros keep both so they don't swap heads.
                                            Press toolsCopper joint in seconds, no flame, water can be in the line. The shop owns it; your job is a clean, square, fully seated pipe at the depth mark.
                                            TrackersA Bluetooth tag in the case of anything over $300 helps you find a stolen tool; it doesn't stop the theft. Locked box, nothing in sight and engraving still do most of the work.

                                            Tool care

                                            So it works on the next job
                                            Press toolWipe it daily. Jaws should open and close with light finger pressure; clean debris out of the jaw profile (a dirty jaw makes a bad press). Silicone-lube the mounting pin. RIDGID RP 330 blinks yellow at 30,000 cycles for service and locks out 2,000 later. Never drop the jaws.
                                            Crimp toolGo/no-go gauge on the first and last crimp every day. Doesn't pass? Adjust per the manual (handles 8¼–8¾" apart at preload on many tools), then recheck. Can't adjust it in = worn out. Oil the linkage.
                                            Expansion toolGrease the cone with the maker's cone grease (it comes in the box) per the manual. Keep heads clean; replace a head with chipped segments.
                                            Tubing cutterKeep the rollers clean; swap a dull or chipped wheel. Tighten a little each turn: cranking down crushes copper out of round.
                                            PEX cutterNew blade when ends come out crushed or angled.
                                            Pipe wrenchBrush the hook jaw teeth, oil the adjusting nut. No cheater bar on an aluminum handle.
                                            PliersA drop of oil in the pivot. Glue and dope off the teeth.
                                            Drills & sawsBlow dust out of the vents. Check cords and chucks. Right blade for the job: bi-metal demo blades find nails.
                                            TorchValve closed and cap on in the truck. Soap-test the fittings. Don't leave cylinders in a hot cab.
                                            Levels & tapeFlip-test levels now and then. Keep the tape blade dry; don't let it slam back.

                                            Batteries in Utah weather

                                            Winter mornings, summer cabs
                                            ColdMilwaukee chargers work about 15–149°F and charge slower below 40°F. A cold pack sags under load: the tool bogs below about 30°F even with bars showing, and the gauge can read high while it charges.
                                            Winter habitBring packs inside overnight. Warm one in the cab before you need it.
                                            HeatA summer dashboard cooks lithium. Keep packs and the charger out of direct sun.
                                            StorageDon't store packs dead for months. Partly charged and cool is easiest on them.
                                            Damaged packsSwollen, cracked or soaked: stop using it. Recycle at a store drop-off, not the trash.

                                            Why traps need vents

                                            The whole DWV idea
                                            The trapHolds 2"–4" of water that blocks sewer gas from the house.
                                            The problemWater falling down a drain pulls air behind it like a plunger. With no air path, that suction siphons the trap dry. Air shoved ahead of a big slug can also blow the seal out.
                                            The fixA vent lets air in and out right near the trap so pressure stays even.
                                            WHYDistance limitOn small pipe at ¼"/ft the max is pipe size × 4 ft (1½" → 6 ft, 2" → 8 ft): that's exactly where the arm has fallen one full pipe diameter. Any farther and the vent opening drops below the trap weir, the arm becomes an S-trap and siphons itself dry.
                                            Gurgle testA drain that gurgles or a trap that keeps losing water usually means a vent problem.

                                            Six ways a trap loses its seal

                                            Sewer smell? Start here
                                            HowWhat happensFix
                                            Self-siphonageThe fixture's own slug fills the arm and pulls the trap empty behind it. Round-bottom lavs and long, steep arms do it.Vent within the trap-arm limit; keep arm slope near ¼"/ft
                                            Induced siphonageAnother fixture's flow rushes past an unvented connection and sucks this trap down.A vent on every trap; don't tie a trap arm in below a busy branch without one
                                            Back pressureAir shoved ahead of a big slug down a stack pushes up through low traps. Water jumps out of a bowl or tub drain.Relief vents, offsets vented right, no fixtures near the base of a tall stack
                                            EvaporationNobody uses it, the water dries up. Basement floor drains, guest showers, vacant houses.Trap primer, deep-seal trap, or pour water in it
                                            Capillary actionHair, string or a rag draped over the weir wicks the water out like a candle wick.Clean the trap
                                            Wind and momentumWind gusting over a vent bounces pressure down the line; a bucket dumped fast carries the seal with it.Vent terminal in clear air and at the right height; a trap that refills (most do)

                                            WHY A trap seal is only 2"–4" of water: 0.07 to 0.14 psi. Any pressure swing bigger than that, either way, moves it. Vents exist to keep the swing smaller than the seal.

                                            Why gas pressure is so small

                                            Inches of water column
                                            The unitA manometer is a U-tube of water. Gas pushes one side down and the other up; the difference in inches is the pressure, written " w.c. (water column).
                                            Conversion1 psi = 27.7" w.c. (because 1 psi holds up 2.31 ft of water, and 2.31 × 12 = 27.7).
                                            A houseNatural gas runs about 7" w.c., roughly ¼ psi. Propane appliances run about 11" w.c.. That's why a psi gauge can't read it: the whole system is under half a psi.
                                            Sizing dropThe common tables allow 0.5" w.c. of loss from meter to appliance, about 7% of 7". Undersize the pipe and the far appliance starves: lazy flame, soot, CO.
                                            2 psi systems2 psi is about 55" w.c. Eight times the pressure lets smaller pipe or CSST carry the same gas, with a regulator dropping it back to 7" before the appliances.
                                            WHYTest pressureThe test (1.5× working, at least 3 psi) is about 12 times what the pipe normally sees. A joint that holds that holds at 7" w.c. for good.

                                            How a toilet flushes

                                            It's a siphon
                                            1 · DumpThe flapper lifts and the tank empties fast through the 2"–3" flush valve, out the rim holes and the siphon jet at the bottom of the bowl.
                                            2 · SiphonThat rush fills the trapway (the S-shaped passage in the china) all the way. A full, falling column pulls the bowl empty behind it: a siphon.
                                            3 · GulpWhen the bowl runs low, air gets into the trapway and breaks the siphon. That gurgle is the flush ending, not a problem.
                                            4 · RefillThe fill valve refills the tank, and its little refill tube tops up the bowl so the trap seal is back.
                                            Weak flush?Tank water set low, rim holes and jet crusted with hard-water scale (common here), a partial clog in the trapway, or a flapper closing early.
                                            WHYLess waterFederal max is 1.6 gal per flush; WaterSense models use 1.28. They lean on a better siphon jet and glazed trapway, so they're pickier about drain slope and long, flat runs.

                                            How a PRV works

                                            A spring against a diaphragm
                                            The balanceA spring pushes the valve open. Downstream water pushes on a diaphragm to close it. Whatever pressure balances the spring is what the house gets.
                                            AdjustingTurn the screw in (clockwise) to raise it, out to lower it: you're squeezing the spring harder. Most come set around 50 psi. Watch a gauge on a hose bibb while you turn, with a faucet running.
                                            CreepA worn seat leaks by. At night, with nothing running, house pressure slowly climbs toward street pressure. Put a gauge with a red max needle on overnight: if the max is way over the setting, the PRV is tired.
                                            Closed systemA PRV stops heated water from pushing back to the street, so the house needs an expansion tank. See Why an expansion tank.
                                            WHYRequiredOver 80 psi static, code wants one. High pressure wears valves, heaters and supply lines and makes hammer worse.

                                            Why metals eat each other

                                            Galvanic corrosion
                                            The batteryTwo different metals touching, with water between them, make a weak battery. Current flows, and the less noble metal dissolves.
                                            Who losesZinc (galvanizing) and steel lose to copper and brass. Copper screwed straight into galvanized eats the steel threads, then it leaks or clogs with rust.
                                            The fixA dielectric union or fitting (a plastic break between the metals), or a brass or bronze fitting between them, as code allows for copper to steel. Water heater nipples are often lined for the same reason.
                                            WHYThe anodeA water heater's anode rod is the same trick on purpose: a more active metal that corrodes so the tank doesn't. Galvanic corrosion, working for you.

                                            Why bigger drains get less slope

                                            ¼" vs ⅛"
                                            GoalWater must move fast enough to carry solids, about 2 ft/s (often called scouring speed).
                                            Big pipeFlows faster at the same slope: more water, less rubbing on the walls for its size. So 3" and up reach scouring speed at ⅛"/ft.
                                            Small pipeMore friction for the water it holds, so it needs the extra push of ¼"/ft.
                                            Too steep?Code sets no maximum. You'll hear "water outruns solids"; the bigger real-world limit is your fall budget: every extra ⅛"/ft costs depth over a long run.

                                            Why the pipe math works

                                            The aha behind the multipliers
                                            Why 1.414A 45° offset is half a square, and a square's diagonal is the side × √2 = 1.414. That's why a framing square's 12 and 12 read 17 across.
                                            Small angle, long travelTravel is the long side: offset ÷ sin(angle). Run is offset ÷ tan(angle). Shrink the angle and sin shrinks, so the multiplier grows and the fitting "lays down" farther.
                                            Rolling = two trianglesThe end view folds roll and set into one true offset, √(roll² + set²). After that it's a plain offset.
                                            Take-off of a sweepCenterlines meet at a virtual corner: take-off = bend radius × tan(angle ÷ 2). Bigger radius, bigger take-off.
                                            Bending gainA bend cuts the corner: the two legs to the corner are longer than the arc. That leftover (about 0.43 × radius on a 90) is why benders have a gain scale.
                                            Area is diameter squaredTwo 2" pipes don't make a 4": it takes four. Friction drops even faster (about diameter to the 4.87), so one size up cuts it to about a quarter.
                                            Mixing is a seesawHot gal × (tank° − mix°) = cold gal × (mix° − cold°). A hotter tank needs less hot per gallon, so a 140° tank behind a mixing valve gives more shower minutes.
                                            Where 0.4085 comes fromgpm × 231 in³ ÷ 60 s ÷ (0.7854 × ID²) ÷ 12 = ft/s. The same chain gives gallons per foot = 0.0408 × ID², the "ID² × 4 per 100 ft" trick.
                                            Top half carries airWet venting, circuit venting and combination waste-and-vent are one trick: the pipe is big enough that it never runs more than half full, so the top half stays an air path. That's why those pipes are upsized and slope-limited (ICC 2021 venting guide).
                                            DFU vs WSFUDFU = load on the drain; WSFU = demand on the supply. Neither is gallons: they're odds-weighted, because fixtures rarely all run at once. Don't mix the two tables.

                                            Why fittings face one way

                                            San tee vs combo
                                            San teeIts short curved turn works when flow drops from horizontal into a vertical pipe: gravity pulls it straight down.
                                            Combo / long sweepGoing vertical to horizontal, falling water hits the turn hard. A long, gradual turn keeps solids moving and stops backups, so use a combo (wye + ⅛ bend) or long sweep.
                                            On its backA san tee laid flat throws flow across into the other branch. Use a wye.

                                            Inside a tank water heater

                                            Know the parts
                                            Dip tubeSends incoming cold water to the bottom so hot water stays on top. A broken one = lukewarm water fast.
                                            Anode rodMagnesium or aluminum rod that corrodes so the steel tank doesn't. Used up = tank starts rusting. Softened water eats it faster; check it every 1–2 years.
                                            Gas valve / thermostatControls the burner (electric heaters use elements).
                                            Flue baffleSlows the hot exhaust so more heat goes into the water.
                                            T&P valveOpens on too much temperature or pressure. Last line of defense, not a pressure control.

                                            Why an expansion tank

                                            PRV = closed system
                                            What happensHeated water expands. In an open system it pushes back to the street main.
                                            The catchA PRV, check valve or backflow device blocks that path. A.O. Smith's test on a 30-gallon heater with nowhere for the water to go: 80 psi at 74°F hit 400 psi by 90°F and 625 psi at 105°F, and the tank bottom started to buckle. That's from warming just 16°F.
                                            SymptomsT&P dripping after heating, banging, bulged or leaking tank, running toilets.
                                            FixAn expansion tank sized for the system, with its air charge set to the house pressure.
                                            WHYThe ahaWater barely compresses, so a cup of expansion has nowhere to go and pressure spikes. The tank's air cushion squeezes instead. Set the air charge with the water off, or the bladder starts out already full.

                                            How a softener works

                                            It's a trade, not a filter
                                            The beadsThe tank is full of plastic resin beads, each covered in spots holding sodium.
                                            The swapCalcium and magnesium (the hardness) stick to those spots tighter than sodium does, so they bump the sodium off. Hard in, soft out, with a little sodium added.
                                            Full upOnce every spot holds calcium, hardness slips through. Time to regenerate.
                                            RegenBackwash (fluff and rinse the bed) → brine draw (salty water flows slow through the beads) → rinse → refill the brine tank for next time. Takes about 1–2 hours; the dirty water goes down the drain.
                                            WHYThe ahaCalcium wins one-on-one, but a flood of sodium wins by numbers: strong brine shoves the calcium back off and reloads the beads. Salt isn't the softener; it's the recharge. That's why more salt per regen buys more capacity, with diminishing returns.

                                            How tankless works

                                            Flow in, fire on
                                            Flow switchWater moving past a flow sensor (about 0.5 gpm) tells the board to light the burner.
                                            ModulatingThe burner throttles (say 13,000 to 199,900 BTU/h) to hit the set temperature at whatever flow you're pulling.
                                            Heat exchangerWater runs through a coil in the flame's path. Condensing units add a second exchanger that pulls heat out of the exhaust until it turns to water.
                                            WHYThe ahaA tankless doesn't have a size in gallons, it has a power limit. gpm = BTU × eff ÷ (500 × rise). Colder incoming water means a bigger rise and fewer gpm: the same heater makes less hot water in a Utah January than in July.
                                            ScaleHardness bakes onto the hot coil like a kettle. Less water to carry the heat → overheating codes. That's what flushing fixes.

                                            Static vs. flowing pressure

                                            Why the gauge lies a little
                                            StaticPressure with nothing running. What a gauge on a hose bibb shows, and what the 80 psi PRV rule is about.
                                            FlowingPressure while water moves. Always lower, because pipe walls and fittings steal some to friction.
                                            WHYThe ahaFriction grows faster than flow: double the gpm and the loss goes up about 3.6× (Hazen-Williams uses flow to the 1.85 power). That's why one shower is fine and the shower plus a flush plus the washer isn't, and why a bigger trunk fixes it.
                                            On the jobA house that reads 65 psi static but has weak showers usually has a friction problem (undersized line, long run, plugged filter or aerator), not a street problem.

                                            How a T&P valve works

                                            Two triggers, one outlet
                                            Pressure sideA spring holds the disc shut until tank pressure beats it: typically 150 psi on a residential heater.
                                            Temperature sideA probe reaching down into the hottest water expands and pushes the valve open at about 210°F.
                                            WHYThe ahaWater above 212°F under pressure stays liquid, then flashes to steam the instant pressure drops: a split tank becomes a steam explosion. Dumping hot water early keeps the tank below boiling. That's why the probe must sit in the tank's top and why nothing (valve, plug, trap) can ever block the discharge line.
                                            DrippingUsually high pressure from thermal expansion, not a bad valve: Mech → Hot water → Expansion tank. Test the lever yearly; if it won't reseat, replace it.

                                            How a frost-free hose bibb works

                                            The valve is inside the house
                                            Long stemThe handle is outside, but the actual seat is 6"–12"+ back inside the heated wall, where it can't freeze.
                                            Drains itselfShut it off and the barrel empties out the spout, so there's no water left in the cold part. That only works if it's pitched down to the outside.
                                            WHYThe ahaLeave a hose on in winter and the barrel can't drain: it freezes and splits inside the wall. Nobody knows until spring, when the first person turns it on and floods the basement.
                                            Vacuum breakerThe cap keeps a hose lying in a bucket of weed killer from being sucked back into the house water.

                                            Why water hammer happens

                                            Bang!
                                            CauseA fast-closing valve stops moving water instantly. Its momentum turns into a pressure spike that slams through the pipe.
                                            Usual suspectsWasher and dishwasher solenoids, quick faucets, high pressure, loose pipe.
                                            FixArrestors (ASSE 1010) at the quick-closing valves, solid pipe support, pressure under 80 psi.

                                            Manifold vs trunk-and-branch

                                            PEX layouts
                                            Home-run manifoldTrunk & branch
                                            LayoutOne line from the manifold to each fixture.Big main line with tees off to fixtures.
                                            ProsSteady pressure when several run, a shutoff per fixture, small lines = faster hot water.Least pipe, simple, familiar.
                                            ConsMost pipe and the most holes in framing.Pressure drops when several run; no central shutoffs.

                                            A hybrid (remote mini-manifolds off a trunk) sits in between.

                                            Freeze protection

                                            Utah winters
                                            Code ideaWater and drain pipe can't be put where it will freeze unless it's protected (IPC 305.4).
                                            Outside wallsKeep supply lines out of exterior walls, unheated attics and garages when you can. If you can't, the pipe goes on the warm side of the insulation.
                                            WHYWhy pipes splitIce takes about 9% more room. The split usually happens between the ice plug and a closed valve, where trapped water has nowhere to go.
                                            Water service6" below the frost line: about 36" along the Wasatch Front (30" frost). See Rough → Drains → Underground.
                                            Hose bibbsFrost-free: the valve seat sits inside the warm wall and the barrel is pitched to drain outside. A hose left on holds water in the barrel and can split it inside the wall.
                                            VentsFrost closure: in very cold areas the roof vent goes up in size (see Rough → Vents → Vent sizing & terminals).
                                            Winter testsWater tests can freeze. Drain them right after inspection, or use the vacuum test (2021 IRC) or Utah's air-test options.

                                            Path to a license

                                            Utah DOPL · R156-55c
                                            LicenseClassroomOJT as apprentice
                                            Residential journeyman432 hr6,000 hr

                                            1 · Apprentice licenseApply to DOPL; employer names your supervisor ($116 on the 2022 form).
                                            SupervisionImmediate supervision, max 3 apprentices per supervisor (R156-55c-401). 4th year: up to 8 hr unsupervised in any 24 (Utah Code 58-55-404, carried into the version effective 1/1/2027).
                                            2 · School + hours≥72 classroom hr per semester at 75%+. Max 3,000 OJT hr per 12 months (R156-55c-302a).
                                            No-school routeJourneyman without the 576 classroom hours takes 16,000 hr as a licensed apprentice: twice the time. School is the shortcut.
                                            MTECH4 yr, 8 semesters, 576 hr. Test-out after semester 4A (6,000 hr) fits the residential path. Confirm which license you're testing for.
                                            3 · ExamsWritten (remote OK) + Utah Plumber Practical in person. Pass, then apply.
                                            Exam realityStatewide pass rates, Jun 2025–Jun 2026: theory 69%, residential theory 56%, practical 55%. The practical is a PVC project and a copper project, 37 points each; the average was 26.2 on PVC and 25.2 on copper, so practice copper. MTECH beat the state: theory 85%, practical 60%. Weak spot statewide: hydronics (DOPL bulletin, May 2026).
                                            4 · RenewEvery 2 yr, Nov 30 of even years: 2026 is a renewal year. 12 CE hr, 8 core.
                                            UpgradeResidential → journeyman: 2,000 hr non-residential + 4th-year class (144 hr).
