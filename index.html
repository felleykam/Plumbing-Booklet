                          Scaling tips

                          From the field
                          Dimensions winMany sets say "Do not scale drawings." Scale to estimate (take-offs, rough locations). Anything that sets a hole, stub or sleeve: find the dimension or ask the super.
                          Measure longErrors grow with the scale, not the length: measure the longest run you can, then divide. A thick wall line can be an inch or more wide at ¼".

                          Venting rules

                          IRC ch. 31 · IPC ch. 9
                          Take-offA vent leaves the drain above the drain's centerline (rolled up, not off the side or bottom) so waste can't run into it.
                          Dry vent riseRise at least 6" above the flood rim of the highest fixture it serves before turning horizontal (IRC P3104.5 / IPC 905.4). Lower than that, it can fill and act as a drain.
                          Common ventTwo fixtures can share one vent: at the same level through a double fixture fitting, or at different levels within the limits (IRC P3107).
                          Wet ventA pipe that vents some fixtures while draining others. Typical house use: horizontal wet venting for up to two bathroom groups on the same floor (IRC P3108). Size it from the wet-vent table.
                          Island sinkIsland vent: the vent loops up as high as possible under the counter, drops, and ties in below the fixture drain, with a cleanout (IRC P3112). Or an AAV where your inspector allows it.
                          AAVListed (ASSE 1051), set upright ≥ 4" above the branch or fixture drain it vents, ≥ 6" above attic insulation, accessible, in a space with air. At least one vent per building still goes outside. Not on sumps or ejectors unless listed for it (IRC P3114).

                          Section numbers are from the 2021 IRC. On layouts, your inspector's call wins.

                          Radon pipe isn't a plumbing vent

                          Utah DEQ guidance
                          What it isA passive radon system: gravel or a collector mat under the slab, and a pipe from there up through a warm part of the house and out the roof. Common in Utah homes, where radon runs high.
                          Never tie inIt carries soil gas. Keep it a separate system: no connection to the DWV, no AAV on it, and don't count it as a vent.
                          Label itWhere it leaves the slab, in closets, in the attic and through the garage, so nobody mistakes it for a plumbing vent or cuts into it.
                          Roof exitBack side of the ridge is best. EPA guide: at least 10 ft above grade and 10 ft from any window or opening less than 2 ft below the outlet. A ¼" screen, not a rain cap.
                          Slab holesSeal around pipes through the slab; sealing alone won't fix radon, but open gaps short-circuit the system.

                          Heat pump water heaters

                          The 2029 federal rule
                          The ruleElectric tank heaters 20–55 gal made after May 6, 2029 must reach UEF 2.30: in practice, a heat pump. Gas tanks only get a small efficiency bump. The gas tankless rule was withdrawn in 2025.
                          AirIt pulls heat from room air: about 700 ft³ of space for most 240V units (some 450), or a louvered door or ducting per the maker. Best in a 50–90°F space; in a cold Utah garage it leans on the backup element.
                          CondensateIt makes water. Plan a condensate drain (or pump) at rough-in, and set the unit level so it drains.
                          ElectricUsually 240V / 30A, like a regular electric tank. 120V plug-in models exist for gas swaps, sized up about two tank sizes.
                          Cold air & noiseBlows cool air and hums like a fridge (≈ 45–55 dB): keep it away from thermostats and bedrooms. Seismic straps still apply.

                          A heat-pump-ready spot = enough air (or a duct path), a drain within reach, and a 240V circuit. Cheap at rough-in, expensive later.

                          Trim out a shower / tub valve

                          Do it in this order
                          1. Flush the lines first. Run water through the valve with the test cap or flush plug off, before the cartridge goes in. Rough-in debris is the #1 cartridge killer.
                          2. Check wall depth. Pull the plaster guard and confirm the finished wall is inside the valve's depth range. Too deep needs the maker's extension kit; too shallow and the handle won't seat.
                          3. Cartridge in, hot on the left. Follow the trim's sheet for orientation and the retaining clip or bonnet nut.
                          4. Set the limit stop. Run full hot, put a thermometer in the stream, set the stop so it tops out at 120°F or less (ASSE 1016 / 1070). WHY The stop limits how far the handle turns, not the temperature. In January the cold line runs about 40°F and cools the mix; in July it's warmer, so the same stop runs hotter. Set a few degrees under 120°F, and recheck if you set it in winter.
                          5. Hot and cold backwards? Fix it at the cartridge, not the handle. Moen 1222: rotate the stem 180°. Others: follow the maker's reversal steps. WHY Flipping just the handle leaves the limit stop on the wrong side, so it no longer caps the hot.
                          6. Tub spout. Match the stub-out to the spout (slip-on vs ½" IPS threaded, length per its sheet). Delta: spout 8"–18" below the valve or you get shower rise (water creeps out the head). Most makers want metal, not PEX, to a diverter spout.
                          7. Escutcheon. Gasket or caulk per the sheet. Many makers say leave the bottom unsealed so any leak shows instead of rotting the wall.
                          8. Test. Every function, every diverter position, look behind the escutcheon for drips, then a flush-test: someone flushes a toilet while you feel the temperature.

                          How to ID any part

                          Do it in this order
                          1. Photo it before you touch it. Logo, front, back, underside, any label, and the part next to a tape measure. Once it's apart, the photos are your only record of how it went together.
                          2. Find the brand. Logo on the handle, cap, escutcheon, spout or body. WHY Brand narrows everything: a Moen cartridge will never fit a Delta valve, even if they look alike.
                          3. Find a number. See Where the number lives below. Numbers like "A112.18.1" or "ASSE 1016" are standards, not model numbers.
                          4. Pull the worn part and read its stamp. Cartridges, stems and flappers are usually stamped. A stamp can be a component number rather than the order number; the maker's tech line can translate it.
                          5. Measure what you can't read. Length, OD, spline count on the stem, thread size (see Threads & sizes). Write it on the photo.
                          6. Search the maker's part finder by model, or call their tech line with the photos, model and serial (Trim → Finders & tech lines).
                          7. Take the old part to the counter. Matching side by side beats any description. Ask for their cartridge or stem identifier book.

                          New construction shortcut: the builder's fixture selection sheet lists every model in the house. Get it once per subdivision and save it in My files.

                              Callback killers

                              Prove it, then protect it
                              Screws & nailsMost simple leak callbacks come from trades who work after you, and the hole may not leak until the house moves. Plate anything under 1¼" from the face, mark plate spots for the drywall crew, photograph your plates. WHY With no plate and a normal-length screw, the plumber usually pays for the repair (Copper Development Assn.).
                              Baseline testBefore cover: gauge photo with the time and psi. Retest at final and compare. That photo settles arguments.
                              Wall photosEvery wall and ceiling after rough, before insulation, keyed to a floor-plan number. Find pipes later without opening drywall.
                              PEX expansion (F1960)Square cut. Ring to the stop. Insert right after the last expansion; you should feel resistance (none = over-expanded).                               PEX crimpRing ⅛"–¼" from the pipe end, tool square to the pipe, jaws fully closed, go/no-go gauge on every crimp. Test-crimp at the start of each job: worn tools crimp loose.
                              FreezeWarm side of the insulation, air leaks sealed. WHY Insulation only slows heat loss; with no heat source the pipe still freezes (ICC).
                              PressureExpansion tank precharge = static house pressure (factory is about 40 psi). Gauging and PRV: Inspect → Utah & cities → Utah water.

                              Secondary water (PI)

                              Very Utah
                              What it isPressurized irrigation: untreated canal or reservoir water for lawns, in its own pipes. Separate box and meter near the front property line.
                              The hard ruleNever connect secondary to culinary (drinking) water, not even with a valve (Utah Code 19-4-112). That's a cross-connection.
                              WHYWhy care?It's untreated and can carry E. coli and fertilizer (Lehi). One wrong hookup and a whole neighborhood's drinking water can get it.
                              Who has itUtah County, confirmed on city sites: Lehi, Saratoga Springs, Eagle Mountain, Spanish Fork, Springville. American Fork likely. Orem: no citywide PI. Elsewhere: ask.
                              Tell them apartPurple = irrigation / non-potable. Blue = potable. Green = sewer (APWA). Springville: purple PE tubing, purple "non-potable" tape about 12" over the pipe, tracer wire, 30" cover, green IRRIGATION box in the park strip.                               Why traps need vents

                              The whole DWV idea
                              The trapHolds 2"–4" of water that blocks sewer gas from the house.
                              The problemWater falling down a drain pulls air behind it like a plunger. With no air path, that suction siphons the trap dry. Air shoved ahead of a big slug can also blow the seal out.
                              The fixA vent lets air in and out right near the trap so pressure stays even.
                              WHYDistance limitOn small pipe at ¼"/ft the max is pipe size × 4 ft (1½" → 6 ft, 2" → 8 ft): that's exactly where the arm has fallen one full pipe diameter. Any farther and the vent opening drops below the trap weir, the arm becomes an S-trap and siphons itself dry.
                              Gurgle testA drain that gurgles or a trap that keeps losing water usually means a vent problem.

                              Why bigger drains get less slope

                              ¼" vs ⅛"
                              GoalWater must move fast enough to carry solids, about 2 ft/s (often called scouring speed).
                              Big pipeFlows faster at the same slope: more water, less rubbing on the walls for its size. So 3" and up reach scouring speed at ⅛"/ft.
                              Small pipeMore friction for the water it holds, so it needs the extra push of ¼"/ft.
                              Too steep?Code sets no maximum. You'll hear "water outruns solids"; the bigger real-world limit is your fall budget: every extra ⅛"/ft costs depth over a long run.

                              Why fittings face one way

                              San tee vs combo
                              San teeIts short curved turn works when flow drops from horizontal into a vertical pipe: gravity pulls it straight down.
                              Combo / long sweepGoing vertical to horizontal, falling water hits the turn hard. A long, gradual turn keeps solids moving and stops backups, so use a combo (wye + ⅛ bend) or long sweep.
                              On its backA san tee laid flat throws flow across into the other branch. Use a wye.

                              Inside a tank water heater

                              Know the parts
                              Dip tubeSends incoming cold water to the bottom so hot water stays on top. A broken one = lukewarm water fast.
                              Anode rodMagnesium or aluminum rod that corrodes so the steel tank doesn't. Used up = tank starts rusting. Softened water eats it faster; check it every 1–2 years.
                              Gas valve / thermostatControls the burner (electric heaters use elements).
                              Flue baffleSlows the hot exhaust so more heat goes into the water.
                              T&P valveOpens on too much temperature or pressure. Last line of defense, not a pressure control.

                              Why an expansion tank

                              PRV = closed system
                              What happensHeated water expands. In an open system it pushes back to the street main.
                              The catchA PRV, check valve or backflow device blocks that path. One maker's example: 80 psi at 74°F climbs to about 310 psi at 90°F with nowhere to go.
                              SymptomsT&P dripping after heating, banging, bulged or leaking tank, running toilets.
                              FixAn expansion tank sized for the system, with its air charge set to the house pressure.
                              WHYThe ahaWater barely compresses, so a cup of expansion has nowhere to go and pressure spikes. The tank's air cushion squeezes instead. Set the air charge with the water off, or the bladder starts out already full.

                              Why water hammer happens

                              Bang!
                              CauseA fast-closing valve stops moving water instantly. Its momentum turns into a pressure spike that slams through the pipe.
                              Usual suspectsWasher and dishwasher solenoids, quick faucets, high pressure, loose pipe.
                              FixArrestors (ASSE 1010) at the quick-closing valves, solid pipe support, pressure under 80 psi.

                              Freeze protection

                              Utah winters
                              Code ideaWater and drain pipe can't be put where it will freeze unless it's protected (IPC 305.4).
                              Outside wallsKeep supply lines out of exterior walls, unheated attics and garages when you can. If you can't, the pipe goes on the warm side of the insulation.
                              WHYWhy pipes splitIce takes about 9% more room. The split usually happens between the ice plug and a closed valve, where trapped water has nowhere to go.
                              Water service6" below the frost line: about 36" along the Wasatch Front (30" frost). See Rough → Drains → Underground.
                              Hose bibbsFrost-free: the valve seat sits inside the warm wall and the barrel is pitched to drain outside. A hose left on holds water in the barrel and can split it inside the wall.
                              VentsFrost closure: in very cold areas the roof vent goes up in size (see Rough → Vents → Vent sizing & terminals).
                              Winter testsWater tests can freeze. Drain them right after inspection, or use the vacuum test (2021 IRC) or Utah's air-test options.

                              Hot water flashesInside a tank, pressure keeps water liquid well past boiling. Once it's out of the pipe, anything hotter than the local boiling point turns to steam.
